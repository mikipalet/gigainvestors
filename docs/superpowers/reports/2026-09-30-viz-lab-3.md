## Five-second reading, density and weaknesses

### 1. Shelf — s

**Five seconds:** “Buy Infosys; expected return 13.1% a year.” Its logo, name and return dominate one green tile. All markets instead leads with Beijing New Building Materials at 16.1%, then outlined tiles for Shanxi Xinghuacun Fen Wine Factory at 15.6% and Infosys at 13.1%. The next tier answers what price reduction would bring each waiting company to its buy price. The remaining companies become a logo wall.

The Western view shows 16 named companies at 1728, 31 at 2056 and seven on the phone, versus seven, seven and six in `m`. It also shows 38 / 47 / five additional logo or initial marks. The all-markets phone retains all three buys, four waiting names and five wall marks. Historical phone buys cap at three, with the fourth behind “+1 more”. Every remainder control opens the appropriate company list.

**Weaknesses:** The hero spends substantial area on one company, and size expresses recommendation rank rather than market value. Secondary price-drop chips require reading; they do not provide a common visual distance scale. Unfamiliar or unavailable logos still need names. At 1728 this is less exhaustive than a tightly packed table, but much more immediately identifiable than `m` with this universe.

### 2. Small multiples — k

**Five seconds:** “Here are the buys and their returns, with a small picture of their price history.” Buy cards lead; waiting companies follow by price / buy price. The leading buy has the sole strong green fill. Each card shows a logo, name, percentage, annual price / historical buy-price line and a pale buy band.

Twenty named cards fit at 1728, 31 at 2056, five on the phone, plus a remainder card. They use ten annual observations, not invented monthly paths. The normalized line makes different currencies comparable. The shared vertical range is 0–4×; cards containing higher observations explicitly say “4× cap”. Missing annual observations break the line. The current playhead moves within the decade; the window shifts when the selected year goes outside it, and future observations are not drawn.

**Weaknesses:** The grid asks the user to compare many miniature charts, so it is slower than the Shelf's single answer. Some cards have sparse historical observations or no expected-return estimate. Capping high multiples compresses extreme histories. This is the strongest alternative for someone who wants immediate historical context, and the most dashboard-like of the four.

### 3. Bullseye — b

**Five seconds:** “The companies inside the green centre are at buying prices.” The separate buy panel makes the exact names and expected percentages immediate. Desktop names beside the centre and inner rings use leader lines where needed; phone names move to the compact buy panel and nearest-company key.

Radius is **linear price / buy price**, with the boundary at 1× and outer rings at 1.5×, 2× and 3×. Sector establishes the angle. Collision avoidance changes angle only within its sector; it never moves a company to a cheaper radius. Farther and crowded companies go to the remainder list. The Western target shows 45 / 52 / 24 distinct company marks across the three sizes; all markets shows 59 / 72 / 26.

**Weaknesses and comprehension risk:** A target is memorable, but it has no familiar “good is left” reading direction. Sector angle can look like another numerical variable. Long leader lines add visual work, and the phone cannot label most marks. The empty centre also makes a buy near the boundary look less decisive than the same company on a Shelf tile. The numeric buy panel is doing much of the comprehension work. This assessment is a screenshot-based five-second reading, not a study with fresh users; the target's standalone comprehension remains unproven.

### 4. Arrivals — a

**Five seconds:** “Infosys moved into the buy zone this year.” The left endpoint is the prior annual snapshot and the right endpoint is the selected year (or today's quote). True buys receive green slopes; other slopes recede. All markets shows the three current buys arriving. The side panel supplies the exact annual-return percentages.

Up to 36 paired slopes fit on desktop and ten on the phone. Labels favor buys, arrivals, departures and the next closest companies. Endpoint positions remain exact; leader lines separate crowded names. The shaded band ends before the label column so a displaced non-buy label cannot appear to sit inside the buy zone. Actual named density is 13 / 18 / five in the Western current view.

**Weaknesses:** Crossing lines and displaced labels take longer to read than tiles. A falling price ratio can reflect a changed buy-price estimate as well as a changed market price; it is not a price-momentum chart. Counts cover the displayed quality cohort with comparable prior-year observations, not a complete ledger of former quality failures. Companies outside the chart's 0–3× range remain in the lists. It is the best secondary Play view, but the weakest default answer.

## Time travel and interaction

All four variants reuse the fiscal-year slider, with Previous, Next and Play/Pause added only to the lab views. Play advances every 900ms. Pointer scrubbing and arrow keys interrupt playback. Query parameters survive stepping and playback.

Measured slow-step animations were **350ms in every variant**, including tile rearrangement, radial motion, slope changes and small-multiple motion. Two changes within 140ms disable motion; it resumes after 180ms idle. The captured fast-scrub checks found `data-fast=true` and zero active animations in all four views. Phone and reduced-motion views also disable animation. The small-multiple playhead independently moved from x=240 to x=213.333 when stepping 2018 → 2017, with a 350ms transition.

Pointer hover and keyboard focus show name, price, buy price and return. Escape dismisses the tooltip. A phone's first tap identifies a company; its second tap opens the dossier. More controls use the existing paginated panel. Separate motion identities prevent a company duplicated in a chart and its buy panel from gliding from the wrong origin.
